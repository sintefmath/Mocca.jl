Cyclic steady state ​

A cyclic process never settles to a constant state: it repeats a cycle of steps. After enough cycles the state of the beds at the end of a cycle equals the state at its start. That is the cyclic steady state, and performance is measured over that cycle. cyclic_steady_state finds it:

julia
result = cyclic_steady_state(process)
result.converged, result.iterations

The cycle maps the state of the beds at its start, , to the state at its end, , and the steady state solves  . The change over a cycle is the largest relative change of any variable of any bed between and (see cycle_change). The page How a VSA cycle works shows the approach to the steady state of one process.

Methods ​

methodHowConverged when
:newton (default)A few plain cycles, then Newton's method on  change per cycle below tol = 10⁻⁹
:andersonRepeated cycles with Anderson accelerationchange per cycle below tol = 10⁻⁶
:mass_balanceRepeated cycleskey component in and out agree within 0.5% for 5 cycles in a row, after at least 50 (Haghpanah et al. 2013; Krishnamurthy et al. 2014)

:newton and :anderson require every variable to settle, including slowly accumulating components such as water. A mass balance on one component does not, and can stop while the water is still far from steady.

How Newton's method gets there ​

Three plain cycles (handover), each from the end of the last, let the fastest changes die out. Newton's method then solves   . Its Jacobian,  , comes from forward sensitivities over one cycle, every column in one sweep, so a Jacobian costs about as much as a cycle on a coarse grid.

Far from the steady state a Newton step can make the change larger before the next ones converge. A step that makes it more than 100 times larger, or from which the cycle fails to simulate, is cut back, up to six times. If Newton's method still does not converge, Anderson acceleration takes the change to 10⁻¹ and then 10⁻³, and Newton's method starts again from each. With handover = 1e-3, Anderson acceleration takes the change to 1e-3 before Newton's method starts, as Mocca 0.2.0 did.

With warm_start (the default), a new run starts from the last steady state found for the process, and its Jacobian. After a small change of a setting it converges in a few iterations, which is what makes sweeps and optimisation quick.

Fine grids ​

Two more things keep Newton's method fast on fine grids:

OptionDefaultEffect
krylov:autoSolves each Newton step by GMRES, one forward sensitivity per product, instead of building the full Jacobian. :auto does so when the beds have more than 800 variables (a bed of about 130 cells with two components, 100 with three); true or false fixes the choice, and a number moves the threshold.
coarse_cells40On a bed of more cells, finds the steady state first on beds of half as many cells, down to 40 or fewer, converged to 10⁻⁶, and starts each finer grid from the coarser one's, interpolated. nothing starts every grid from its initial state.

The coarser grids only give the start; the steady state is that of the process as set up. If Newton's method does not converge from a coarser grid's steady state, the fine grid is started from its initial state.

The full list, and the lower-level solvers, are in the docstrings of cyclic_steady_state, newton_cyclic_steady_state, simulate_to_cyclic_steady_state and simulate_until_mass_balance.

Each bar shows how far the smallest change so far has come from the first cycle of its phase to the phase's tolerance, on a log scale, and the estimate of what is left is for that phase. From the last steady state of the process, Newton's method starts at once, with a single line. With method = :mass_balance the line shows the balance error of the key component instead, and how many cycles in a row it has been below the tolerance.

Pass progress = false to turn it off, or progress = true to have it in a script, as the examples do. Outside a terminal, such as in a log file, each cycle gets its own line. It is off when optimise! finds the steady state of each design.
