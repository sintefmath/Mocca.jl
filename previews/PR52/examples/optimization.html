Optimization of a cyclic process ​

We optimise the four-stage vacuum swing adsorption (VSA) cycle of Haghpanah et al. (2013) at cyclic steady state, twice over:

  1. maximise the CO₂ recovery, keeping the purity of the CO₂ product at 90% or above;

  2. from that design, minimise the energy per tonne of CO₂ captured, keeping the purity at 90% and the recovery at 60% or above.

Each evaluation finds the cyclic steady state, starting from the last one, and its gradient with respect to the design variables from adjoints (see the gradients example). A gradient-based optimiser (BFGS within the bounds) then needs a few tens of evaluations, where the genetic algorithm of the paper evaluates thousands. For more details about the VSA modelling, see the process from its parts example.

julia
import Mocca
using Mocca.Units
using CairoMakie

The process and its design variables ​

Four of the decision variables of section 7.1.1 of the paper: the feed velocity, the evacuation pressure P_L, the blowdown pressure P_I and the adsorption time. The bounds of the pressures and the velocity are those of Table 5 of the paper. The paper also varies the blowdown and evacuation times, which we leave out to keep the example short.

Since a duration is among the variables, a stage of a different duration keeps the time steps it has now, stretched in proportion, so that the performance changes smoothly with the duration.

julia
process = Mocca.four_stage_vsa_process()
Mocca.add_variable!(process, :v_feed, ("adsorption", :velocity); bounds = (0.1, 2.0), description = "interstitial feed velocity [m/s]")
Mocca.add_variable!(process, :P_L, ("evacuation", :to); bounds = (0.05bar, 0.5bar), description = "evacuation pressure")
Mocca.add_variable!(process, :P_I, ("blowdown", :to); bounds = (0.05bar, 0.5bar), description = "blowdown pressure")
Mocca.add_variable!(process, :t_ads, ("adsorption", :duration); bounds = (10.0, 100.0), description = "adsorption time [s]")

The blowdown must end above the evacuation pressure, which the optimiser takes as a linear constraint on the variables, P_L − P_I ≤ −0.01 bar:

julia
blowdown_above_evacuation = [(Dict(:P_L => 1.0, :P_I => -1.0), -0.01bar)]
1-element Vector{Tuple{Dict{Symbol, Float64}, Float64}}:
 (Dict(:P_I => -1.0, :P_L => 1.0), -1000.0)

julia
show_design(values) = join(["$k = " * (k in (:P_L, :P_I) ? "$(round(v / bar, digits = 3)) bar" : "$(round(v, sigdigits = 3))")

The evacuation pressure reaches its lower bound within ten evaluations: a deeper vacuum takes more CO₂ off the adsorbent, so the recovery rises as P_L falls. Haghpanah et al. find the same (their Figure 11), and reach 90% purity and recovery together only with P_L ≤ 0.02 bar. The blowdown pressure then sets the purity: blowing down further removes more of the nitrogen, but loses more CO₂ with it.

2. Minimise the energy ​

The optimiser starts from the design above, where the process was left. Each target is a penalty of weight (1000 by default) times the square of the shortfall, so the objective must be of order one: with the energy in units of 100 kWh/t, a shortfall of 1 percentage point of recovery costs as much as 10 kWh/t.

julia
min_energy = Mocca.optimise!(process; minimise = :specific_energy, at_least = (purity = 0.9, recovery = 0.6),
    scale = (specific_energy = 100.0,), linear_constraints = blowdown_above_evacuation, max_it = 15, log = report)
println("$(min_energy.evaluations) evaluations: ", show_design(min_energy.values))
println(show_performance(min_energy.metrics))

fig

Minimising the energy gives up five points of recovery for about a quarter less energy per tonne. It does not raise the evacuation pressure, which stays at its bound: it blows down less deeply and feeds for longer and faster, so that the pump work is spread over more CO₂ captured per cycle. That also raises the productivity, so the same plant would need less adsorbent.
